## The idea under both: the book already wrote the film

The chapters talk to the person wearing the headset. They call you the Reader. Their story is a small fox becoming the lighthouse's keeper, and its light keeps getting out of the book and into your room. Some of the lines, in the order they're heard (each is in `client/public/show/episode-*.json`):

- "Every night, the lighthouse at the edge of the table sent its light out over the house, and every lost thing followed it home." (chapter 1's first line)
- "Can I trim the wick tonight, Wick? Please?" / "When you're bigger, lad." (chapter 1's opening)
- "It's lit! It was cold, and then you held it, and it just... woke up." (Pip, when you hand the lantern back)
- "Someone helped me. Someone very big." / "Hmph. Well. Thank you kindly, whoever you are." (the far side)
- "And the little light became the big one, and swept out over every lost thing in the house." (the beam crosses your walls)
- "Not bad, keeper." / "Keeper?" (chapter 1's end)
- "That night, the sea came in further than it ever had. Up the shore, up the rocks, and right across the Reader's floor." (chapter 2)
- "And just then, the snow stopped, the clouds opened, and the whole sky came out." … "They say every keeper gets a star. Looks like that one's yours." (chapter 3's end)
- "Same time tomorrow, keeper?" / "Same time tomorrow."

So neither film needs a voice-over of ours, a slogan, or a feature card: the book narrates its own film. That's the main protection against the owner's "AI slop" and "forced". Every word heard is a line a judge will hear in the headset, and every image is something they can open tonight. **Lines are never altered or re-voiced.** A beat may use only some of a moment's lines, cut between whole lines, never inside one.

## Concept A: "Out over the house" (recommended)

**Logline.** A small fox wants to be the lighthouse's keeper, and a pair of very big hands help him. Each time the lamp is lit, its light gets further out of the book: across your walls, over your floor, up to your ceiling. Then the book closes, and the lights come up on a room that is just a room again.

**The feeling we want left behind.** Wonder that grows, and then a small ache when the lights come up: *I want to sit at that table tonight.* Not "what a lot of features", but "the room I'm sitting in could do that, and that little fox needed me."

**Why it fits.**
- It's the owner's "larger than life", told literally. The film's scale climbs from a lantern in your fingers to a ceiling of stars. Each step is a room effect the book really does, in the order the chapters do them.
- It has one causal thread, so the effects don't turn into a montage. "When you're bigger, lad" is paid off by "Not bad, keeper", and then by the keeper's star.
- It answers the judges' "the player's real room should meaningfully change the experience" without saying so.
- It shows the must-passes (hands only, seated, one mechanic done well: hand them what they need) inside the story, not as a checklist.

**The visual grammar.** The camera is your eyes, so the film moves by *looking*. From the book to your hand, along the beam to the wall, down to the floor, up to the ceiling, and back to the book. Each look is slow and held, and the looks do the work of cuts.

### What a judge takes from each beat

- **1–2:** The first image is the strangest true one we have: a light from a book a hand tall, moving on an ordinary wall. Starting on the book means it can't read as a lamp in the room. The want is set in two lines: Pip wants to be the keeper.
- **4, 6:** The one mechanic, done well and done twice: hand them what they need. Hands only, seated, close, and each one allowed to finish.
- **5:** Parallax, the thing a screen can't give. One slow lean.
- **7:** The film's turn. The characters know you're there.
- **8–10:** The room in three steps (walls, floor, ceiling), each named by the book as it happens. Each step is paid off with "keeper". This is the "larger than life".
- **11–12:** The vision, shown instead of promised: the same cast making up a new story from one spoken sentence.
- **13–14:** The reason to come back, said by the characters; then the plain facts.

## Concept B: "Someone very big"

**Logline.** A small fox afraid of the dark, a lantern, and a pair of enormous, careful hands: three nights in a storybook, seen from inside the headset and, three times, from across the room. It's about what it feels like to be the one the story is waiting for.

**The feeling we want left behind.** Tenderness. The judge should be moved by a gesture: a grown-up at a kitchen table cupping nothing in their palm, while inside the headset a lantern relights in it.

**Why it's different from A.** A is about scale and the room. B is about the hands and the relationship. The cast keep speaking to the Reader ("You came back!", "Is that the Reader? The one who reaches down from the sky?", "Much obliged, Reader. Truly."), and the film is built from those moments. Its device is **two views of one moment**: a still tripod shot of the real person at the real table, then the same moment in the headset. It shows seated play, the two-foot reach and hands-only more plainly than any POV shot can.

**Structure.** Four short movements, each opened by a hand-set card. Two cards are ours: *The first night.* and *And any night after.* Two echo the chapters' own openings: *The next night.* (chapter 2's first words) and *The longest night.* (chapter 3's "On the longest night of the year").

### B's risks, which are why it isn't recommended

- **It breaks this lane's own footage brief.** The brief is "only footage we can actually capture on the live build", and Devpost asks for "footage of your Project as viewed on a Meta VR device". Nothing in the competition's rules bans a few seconds of the player filmed from outside. Meta's own store guidance recommends "a mixture of traditional shots, mixed reality, and first person POV", and its A/B tests found trailers showing the player raised conversions by 25–30%. But a tripod shot isn't footage *of the project*. B needs the owner to relax the brief for these three inserts; without them it becomes a weaker A. Only entrants may appear (§4), so the person in frame is the owner.
- **It needs a second recording, synced.** A phone on a tripod running while the headset records, matched by a clap. Doable in one sitting, but one more thing to go wrong on Nov 18.
- **"Person in a headset waving at air" is a known trailer cliché** and can look silly. It works only if the shots are still, wide, lit like a painting and very short: three of them, never more.
- **Its strongest beat (7) depends on the hunt,** which hasn't been walked in the headset yet.

## Recommendation: A, "Out over the house"

A is the one I'd film:
- It needs only what the owner can capture alone, in one sitting, in the headset.
- It doesn't lean on relaxing the brief, or on the unproven hunt.
- Its climb from a lantern to a ceiling is the "larger than life" the owner asked for, earned shot by shot instead of claimed.
- It borrows B's best moment ("Someone very big"), and the keeper thread gives it B's heart without B's risks.

Codex's review agreed: A is the sounder production choice, and B has the clearer emotional premise. The revisions above (the keeper thread, one fewer effect, the quiet close) are aimed at that gap.

**Requests for the Storyteller (S9).** Each is short and in the owner's own words. Nothing branded, no real names, never the teapot keepsake.
- "Tonight's story is about a paper crane who's never seen the sea."
- "Tonight's story is about a button that wants to be the moon."
- "Tonight's story is about a lost mitten looking for its other half."

Keep the take where the cast's reply is clearest. The painting takes up to about 30 s to arrive. It is shown only as a later moment, after a dissolve, never as if it were instant, and only framed without its "Powered by Runway" line. If it can't be framed that way, it's left out, and the spoken exchange carries the beat.

**The room for every shot.**
- One table, plain walls, a real window in view for the beam to cross.
- Clear floor ahead for the sea, and a ceiling without a fan or a bright fitting in the middle.
- A practical lamp or two, warm and out of frame.
- Nothing branded anywhere: no screens, no books with readable spines, no boxes or logos.
- **The room light, Dark or Lighter** (the book's Settings): Lighter stops the dim partway, which helps the walls read on video but weakens the beam's contrast. Stage 2 films a test of both before choosing.

## Sound

- **Only the book's own sound.** That means the narrator, the cast, the foley (paper, brass, felt) and the room effects' own sounds, all captured with the app. No added voice-over, no stock trailer music, no whooshes, no risers.
- **The music bed only if `bed.mp3` clears its Content ID test** (`art/sound/music/LICENCE.md`). If it clears, it stays at the level the app plays it, lifted a few dB only under beat 8 and the end card. If not, the film has none.
- **Room tone** under the cards and the cuts, recorded in the same room (S11), so no cut drops to digital silence.
- **The owner's one sentence** in beat 12 is the only human voice in the film.
- **A known mismatch.** The chapter voices (prepared with ElevenLabs) and the Storyteller's live voices (OpenAI) are different voices for the same characters. Beat 11's card and the cut to "another evening" carry the change. A judge will hear the difference in the headset anyway, so we don't hide it.

## Text cards and captions

- **Three cards in the whole film,** all hand-set on the book's own art, never on a template:
  - the title on the cover's empty plate (beat 3);
  - *Or tell them tonight's story.* on the page's paper (beat 11);
  - the end card on the cover (beat 14).

  Fraunces for the name, Atkinson Hyperlegible Next for everything else, in the book's ink, page and lamp-gold colours (`docs/design-language.md`). Sentence case, no exclamation marks, no feature names.
- **Captions: one consistent track, the whole way through.** The book's own printed caption is small and often too far off in a wide shot. So every spoken line also gets a film caption, styled like the book's caption band: ink-coloured Atkinson Hyperlegible Next on a narrow strip of page-coloured paper, low in the frame, so it's legible on any passthrough. A complete `.srt` goes on YouTube as well.

## Risks the stage 2 plan has to clear first

- **The app's layer and sound in the recording.** Two earlier in-headset recordings had passthrough only, or no voices. Stage 2 tests the capture methods (in-headset recording, casting, Meta's Developer Hub) on a test clip before any filming.
- **Resolution.** In-headset recording is 1080p at about 36 fps. Meta's Developer Hub can record 2160p at 60. Stage 2 tests which works for a WebXR page with its sound.
- **Dim rooms film badly.** The room dim and evening light make passthrough noisy on video. Warm practical lamps help, and so does a test of Dark against Lighter.
- **Head motion.** POV footage magnifies every wobble. Meta's capture guide says to set image stabilization to Low, move the head slowly, and act "as the camera operator". Every look starts and ends with a two-second hold and is taken three times.
- **Timings.** Lines can't be shortened. The beat sheet is an estimate until the real takes are timed (stage 3).

## The Devpost copy: direction

### Tagline (≤140 characters)

Recommended, 134 characters:

> A painted pop-up book opens on your table. Help a small fox keep the lighthouse lit, and its sea, snow and stars spill into your room.

It's concrete (a fox, a lighthouse), gives the reader one verb ("help"), and makes the mixed-reality claim as an image rather than an adjective. Two alternates:

- "A painted pop-up storybook for your real table. Hand a frightened fox his lantern, and the lighthouse's beam sweeps across your walls." (134)
- Lane E's current: "A painted pop-up storybook opens on your real table. Reach in, hand the characters what they need, and watch your room join the story." (134) It's accurate, but "the characters" and "join the story" are generic: it could describe any storybook.

### The description's shape (revised for the demo)

**What the rules require** (§4): a text description covering:
- the **inspiration**;
- **how we built it**;
- **future plans for improvement**;
- a **target launch date**: spring 2027, on the Meta Horizon Store and Meta VR Glasses.

**The form's fields:**

So "Inspiration" comes back as its own short heading, and it is the one real reason, said plainly: *on a screen you only watch; a book on your table needs your hands.* The order, in the demo's plain register:
1. A one-scene opening.
2. What it is.
3. Inspiration.
4. How a reader moves through it, in the film's order.
5. The Storyteller.
6. How we built it.
7. What's next and when.

Everywhere, "we built" and "it does" beat "it delivers" or "it reimagines".

The earlier guidance, which still applies within that order: keep the headings Devpost's template expects, but lead with a moment instead of a thesis, and write it the way we'd explain it to a friend at the table. About 500 words:

1. **One scene first (2–3 sentences).** Pip's lantern rolls to the edge of your table, and he looks up at you. You reach in and hand it back, and it relights in his paws. That's Foldtales.
2. **What it is (3–4 sentences).** A pop-up storybook for the evening, opened from a link in Quest Browser. Three chapters, six minutes each, hands only, with no score and no losing. The story plays to its end if you just watch.
3. **The three nights, each with its room effect (one short paragraph).** The beam on your walls, the sea across your floor, the snow on your table and the stars on your ceiling. Tie each to what happens in the story, which is why it's there.
4. **The Storyteller, honestly (3 sentences).** What the AI does: it improvises a new story with the same cast, answers out loud, paints what you ask for, and can bring weather into the room. What it doesn't: it never touches the written chapters, and the app ends the story if the AI is slow.
5. **How it's made, honestly (3–4 sentences).** Painted with AI from our own designs, then cut out, finished and rigged by hand. Puppets at twelve poses a second. Built on IWSDK, so it opens from a link. One specific detail of craft per sentence.
6. **What's next (2 sentences).** More chapters, Meta VR Glasses in spring 2027, Story Circle. No adjectives.

An opening in that voice, to show the register (not final copy):

> Pip's lantern has rolled to the edge of your kitchen table, and he's looking up at you. You reach in, pinch it, and hand it back, and it lights again in his paws. "It was cold, and then you held it, and it just... woke up."
